Is Friston's Free Energy Principle Just Cross-Entropy Loss?

On the surprising mathematical parallels between neuroscience and machine learning

December 7, 2025

Berekly, Descartes, Kant, Schopenhaeur, and so on, over and over, this isn't anything new. the map is not the territort. but what is it then that we are experiencing? what is our brain doing and why? Karl Friston's Free Energy Principle (FEP) has been called one of the most ambitious theories in neuroscience. It claims that all adaptive systems, from single cells to entire brains, work by minimizing a single quantity called "free energy." Looking at the math fundamental to its theory, I just thought of cross-entropy loss.

What is the Free Energy Principle?

Friston's Free Energy Principle states that biological systems maintain their existence by minimizing variational free energy, a quantity borrowed from statistical physics and information theory. In the FEP framework, your brain builds an internal generative model of the world and constantly updates it to minimize surprise (or equivalently, prediction error).

The free energy $F$ is defined as:

$$F = \mathbb{E}_{q(z)}[\log q(z) - \log p(x, z)]$$

where $q(z)$ is your brain's approximate posterior (its best guess about hidden causes $z$ given sensory data $x$), and $p(x,z)$ is the true joint distribution of observations and their causes.

This can be rewritten as:

$$F = D_{KL}(q(z) \| p(z|x)) - \log p(x)$$

The first term is the KL divergence between your approximate posterior and the true posterior (how wrong your beliefs are). The second term is the log evidence (how surprising the data is). Since $\log p(x)$ doesn't depend on $q$, minimizing $F$ with respect to $q$ is equivalent to minimizing the KL divergence, making your beliefs as accurate as possible.

The ELBO Connection

If you've studied variational inference or Variational Autoencoders (VAEs), this should look extremely familiar. The free energy $F$ is exactly the negative of the Evidence Lower BOund (ELBO):

$$\text{ELBO} = -F = \mathbb{E}_{q(z)}[\log p(x|z)] - D_{KL}(q(z) \| p(z))$$

The ELBO decomposes into two terms:

  • Accuracy/Reconstruction: $\mathbb{E}_{q(z)}[\log p(x|z)]$ — how well your model explains the data
  • Complexity/Regularization: $-D_{KL}(q(z) \| p(z))$ — how much your posterior deviates from the prior

Maximizing the ELBO (equivalently, minimizing free energy) means finding a balance between fitting the data well and not making your model too complex. This is exactly what VAEs do during training.

Cross-Entropy Loss: A Special Case

What about cross-entropy loss, the standard objective for classification neural networks? Cross-entropy minimizes:

$$\mathcal{L}_{CE} = -\sum_{i} y_i \log \hat{y}_i$$

where $y_i$ is the true label distribution and $\hat{y}_i$ is the predicted distribution. This can be rewritten as:

$$\mathcal{L}_{CE} = H(y, \hat{y}) = H(y) + D_{KL}(y \| \hat{y})$$

Since the entropy $H(y)$ of the true labels is constant, minimizing cross-entropy is equivalent to minimizing the KL divergence between the true and predicted distributions. This is structurally identical to what the Free Energy Principle does—minimize the divergence between your beliefs and reality.

MSE: Not Quite the Same

What about Mean Squared Error (MSE)? At first glance, MSE seems different:

$$\mathcal{L}_{MSE} = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(y_i - \hat{y}_i)^2$$

But MSE is actually the negative log-likelihood under a Gaussian assumption. If you assume your prediction errors are Gaussian-distributed:

$$p(y|x) = \mathcal{N}(y; f(x), \sigma^2)$$

Then minimizing MSE is equivalent to maximizing log-likelihood (or equivalently, minimizing negative log-likelihood). So MSE is a special case of variational free energy minimization when you assume Gaussian observation noise.

Prediction Error and Predictive Coding

Friston's framework goes beyond static inference—it includes predictive coding, a theory of how the brain might implement free energy minimization hierarchically. In predictive coding, each layer of the brain predicts the activity of the layer below, and only prediction errors (mismatches between predictions and actual signals) propagate upward.

This is remarkably similar to backpropagation in neural networks, where gradients (which measure prediction error with respect to parameters) flow backward through layers. The difference is that in predictive coding, errors flow in both directions—upward to update beliefs about hidden causes, and downward to refine predictions.

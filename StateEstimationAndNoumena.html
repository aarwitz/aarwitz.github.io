Control Theory and Kant's Noumenon

December 10, 2025

I was reading about state estimation when I came across this line:

"There is a real physical state of an object: \(x_k\). You never know this exactly — you only estimate it."

This made me think of Kant's distinction between noumenon (the thing-in-itself) and phenomenon (the thing-as-it-appears-to-us), and the saying, "The map is not the territory". Regardless of the sensor used, whether a depth sensor or human eyes, there is always noise contributing to a residual error. Kant would say that we, or any sensing thing, has some structure with which it takes hold of the universe to observe it. He posited, arguably incorrectly, that even space and time themselves were artifacts of our cognitive framework.

So, I think Kant would like our approach to state estimation. In state estimation, we're developing algorithms by explicitly acknowledging they'll never access the true state \(x_k\). In state estimation, \(x_k\) is the noumenon. It's the true position of the robot, the actual depth of an object, the real velocity of the car. It exists independent of our sensors. But we can never directly access it. What we have instead is:

  • Measurements \(z_k\): noisy sensor readings (the phenomena)
  • Estimates \(\hat{x}_k\): our best guess of the true state
  • Uncertainty \(P_k\): how confident we are in our estimate

The Kalman filter doesn't try to "know" \(x_k\) with certainty. It maintains a probability distribution over possible states. It says: "Given what I've measured, here's what I believe the state is, and here's how uncertain I am."

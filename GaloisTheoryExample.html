Understanding Galois Theory Through $x^2 - 2$

A step-by-step walkthrough

Let's understand how Galois theory determines whether a polynomial has a closed-form solution by working through the example $f(x) = x^2 - 2$ from the ground up.

Building Blocks: Key Definitions

What is a Group?

A group is a set $G$ together with a binary operation $\circ$ that satisfies four properties:

  1. Closure: For any $a, b \in G$, we have $a \circ b \in G$
  2. Associativity: $(a \circ b) \circ c = a \circ (b \circ c)$
  3. Identity element: There exists $e \in G$ such that $e \circ a = a \circ e = a$ for all $a \in G$
  4. Inverse element: For each $a \in G$, there exists $a^{-1} \in G$ such that $a \circ a^{-1} = a^{-1} \circ a = e$

Example: The integers under addition

$(\mathbb{Z}, +)$ is a group:

  • Set: $\mathbb{Z} = \{\ldots, -2, -1, 0, 1, 2, \ldots\}$
  • Operation: Addition $(+)$
  • Identity: $0$ (since $a + 0 = a$)
  • Inverse: For any integer $a$, its inverse is $-a$ (since $a + (-a) = 0$)

What is a Subgroup?

A subgroup $H$ of a group $G$ is a subset $H \subseteq G$ that is itself a group under the same operation.

Key requirement: A subgroup must always contain the identity element of $G$.

Example: Even integers

The even integers $2\mathbb{Z} = \{\ldots, -4, -2, 0, 2, 4, \ldots\}$ form a subgroup of $\mathbb{Z}$ under addition:

  • Contains identity: $0 \in 2\mathbb{Z}$
  • Closed: Even + Even = Even
  • Inverses exist: $-2a$ is also even

What is an Automorphism?

An automorphism is a structure-preserving map from a mathematical object to itself. For fields, it's a bijective function that preserves addition and multiplication.

In the context of polynomials, automorphisms permute the roots while keeping the polynomial's algebraic structure (coefficients and their relationships) unchanged.

What is a Normal Subgroup?

A subgroup $N$ of $G$ is normal (written $N \triangleleft G$) if for every $g \in G$ and $n \in N$, we have $gng^{-1} \in N$.

Intuitively: $N$ is "invariant under conjugation" by elements of $G$.

What is a Solvable Group?

A group $G$ is solvable if it has a chain of subgroups

$$\{e\} = G_0 \triangleleft G_1 \triangleleft G_2 \triangleleft \cdots \triangleleft G_n = G$$

where each $G_i$ is a normal subgroup of $G_{i+1}$, and each quotient $G_{i+1}/G_i$ is abelian (commutative).

Think of it as: "Can we break down $G$ into simpler, commutative pieces?"

Working Through $f(x) = x^2 - 2$

Step 1: Find the Roots

The polynomial $f(x) = x^2 - 2$ has two roots:

$$r_1 = \sqrt{2}, \quad r_2 = -\sqrt{2}$$

Step 2: Identify the Automorphisms (Symmetries)

We need to find all permutations of the roots that leave the polynomial unchanged (automorphisms of the splitting field).

There are exactly two such permutations:

1. Identity ($\text{id}$):

$$\begin{aligned} \text{id}(\sqrt{2}) &= \sqrt{2} \\ \text{id}(-\sqrt{2}) &= -\sqrt{2} \end{aligned}$$

Leaves everything unchanged.

2. Swap ($\sigma$):

$$\begin{aligned} \sigma(\sqrt{2}) &= -\sqrt{2} \\ \sigma(-\sqrt{2}) &= \sqrt{2} \end{aligned}$$

Swaps the two roots. Corresponds to the map $x \mapsto -x$.

Why does this preserve the polynomial?
If we substitute $-x$ into $f(x) = x^2 - 2$, we get $f(-x) = (-x)^2 - 2 = x^2 - 2 = f(x)$. The polynomial is unchanged!

Step 3: Form the Galois Group

The Galois group $G = \text{Gal}(f)$ is the set of all these automorphisms:

$$G = \{\text{id}, \sigma\}$$

This group has two elements. Let's verify it's actually a group:

  • Closure: Composing any two elements gives another element in $G$
  • Identity: $\text{id}$ is the identity
  • Inverses: $\sigma \circ \sigma = \text{id}$, so $\sigma$ is its own inverse
  • Associativity: Function composition is always associative

Composition table:

$\circ$ $\text{id}$ $\sigma$
$\text{id}$ $\text{id}$ $\sigma$
$\sigma$ $\sigma$ $\text{id}$

Note: This group is isomorphic to $\mathbb{Z}_2$ (integers modulo 2) or $C_2$ (cyclic group of order 2).

Step 4: Find All Subgroups

What are all possible subgroups of $G = \{\text{id}, \sigma\}$?

Remember: A subgroup must:

  • Contain the identity element
  • Be closed under the group operation
  • Contain inverses of all its elements

Subgroup 1: $H_0 = \{\text{id}\}$

The trivial subgroup (just the identity).

Subgroup 2: $H_1 = \{\text{id}, \sigma\} = G$

The whole group.

Why isn't $\{\sigma\}$ a subgroup?

Because it doesn't contain the identity! Also, $\sigma \circ \sigma = \text{id}$, which means composing elements in $\{\sigma\}$ gives us $\text{id}$, which is not in $\{\sigma\}$. So it's not closed under composition.

Step 5: Check if Subgroups are Normal

For each subgroup $H$, we need to check if $gHg^{-1} = H$ for all $g \in G$.

$H_0 = \{\text{id}\}$ is normal:

For any $g \in G$: $g \cdot \text{id} \cdot g^{-1} = g \cdot g^{-1} = \text{id} \in H_0$ ✓

$H_1 = G$ is normal:

The whole group is always a normal subgroup of itself. ✓

Pro tip: In abelian groups (where all elements commute), every subgroup is automatically normal because $gHg^{-1} = gg^{-1}H = H$.

Step 6: Build the Subnormal Series

Now we can construct a chain of normal subgroups:

$$\{\text{id}\} \triangleleft G$$

This gives us one quotient group to check: $G / \{\text{id}\} \cong G$.

Step 7: Check if Quotients are Abelian

For $G$ to be solvable, each quotient $G_{i+1}/G_i$ must be abelian.

Our only quotient is $G/\{\text{id}\} \cong G = \{\text{id}, \sigma\}$.

Is this abelian? Let's check if $\text{id} \circ \sigma = \sigma \circ \text{id}$:

$$\begin{aligned} \text{id} \circ \sigma &= \sigma \\ \sigma \circ \text{id} &= \sigma \\ \therefore \text{id} \circ \sigma &= \sigma \circ \text{id} \quad \checkmark \end{aligned}$$

Yes! The quotient is abelian.

✓ Conclusion: The Group is Solvable!

Since we found a chain of normal subgroups $\{\text{id}\} \triangleleft G$ where the quotient $G/\{\text{id}\}$ is abelian, the Galois group is solvable.

By Galois Theory:

The polynomial $x^2 - 2$ has a closed-form solution expressible using radicals.

$$x = \pm\sqrt{2}$$

Why This Matters

The Big Picture

Galois theory gives us a way to determine a priori (before actually solving) whether a polynomial equation has a closed-form solution.

  • Solvable Galois group → Closed-form solution exists using radicals ($+, -, \times, \div, \sqrt[n]{\cdot}$)
  • Non-solvable Galois group → No closed-form solution using radicals

For Higher Degree Polynomials

The same process applies to polynomials of any degree:

  • Degree 2, 3, 4: Galois groups are always solvable → Quadratic, cubic, quartic formulas exist
  • Degree 5+: Galois groups can be non-solvable (e.g., $S_5$, the symmetric group) → No general formula exists

Key Takeaway

The structure of how roots can be permuted while preserving the polynomial (the Galois group) directly determines whether we can write down an explicit formula for those roots. This deep connection between symmetry and solvability is one of the most beautiful results in mathematics.

Understanding Galois Theory Through $x^2 - 2$

A step-by-step walkthrough

Let's understand how Galois theory determines whether a polynomial has a closed-form solution by working through the example $f(x) = x^2 - 2$.

Building Blocks: Key Definitions

What is a Group?

A group is a set $G$ together with a binary operation $\circ$ that satisfies four properties:

  1. Closure: For any $a, b \in G$, we have $a \circ b \in G$
  2. Associativity: $(a \circ b) \circ c = a \circ (b \circ c)$
  3. Identity element: There exists $e \in G$ such that $e \circ a = a \circ e = a$ for all $a \in G$
  4. Inverse element: For each $a \in G$, there exists $a^{-1} \in G$ such that $a \circ a^{-1} = a^{-1} \circ a = e$

Example: The integers under addition

$(\mathbb{Z}, +)$ is a group:

  • Set: $\mathbb{Z} = \{\ldots, -2, -1, 0, 1, 2, \ldots\}$
  • Operation: Addition $(+)$
  • Identity: $0$ (since $a + 0 = a$)
  • Inverse: For any integer $a$, its inverse is $-a$ (since $a + (-a) = 0$)

What is a Subgroup?

A subgroup $H$ of a group $G$ is a subset $H \subseteq G$ that is itself a group under the same operation.

Every subgroup must contain the identity and be closed under the group operation and inverses.

Example: Even integers

The even integers $2\mathbb{Z} = \{\ldots, -4, -2, 0, 2, 4, \ldots\}$ form a subgroup of $\mathbb{Z}$ under addition:

  • Contains identity: $0 \in 2\mathbb{Z}$
  • Closed: Even + Even = Even
  • Inverses exist: $-2a$ is also even

What is a Coset?

A coset is a "shifted copy" of a subgroup. Let $G$ be a group and $H \subseteq G$ a subgroup. For any $g \in G$:

Left coset:
$$gH = \{gh : h \in H\}$$
Right coset:
$$Hg = \{hg : h \in H\}$$

Think of $gH$ as the subgroup $H$ translated so that its "identity" sits at $g$ instead of at the group identity. Cosets have the same size as $H$ and play a key role in counting and quotient constructions. The set of all left cosets of $H$ in $G$ is denoted $G/H$.

Example: Integers mod 3

Let $G = \mathbb{Z}$ and $H = 3\mathbb{Z} = \{\ldots, -3, 0, 3, 6, \ldots\}$.

The left cosets are:

  • $0 + 3\mathbb{Z}$
  • $1 + 3\mathbb{Z}$
  • $2 + 3\mathbb{Z}$

There are only three left (or right) cosets because we do not include duplicates. For example, $3 + 3\mathbb{Z} = 0 + 3\mathbb{Z}$.
For this reason, it is said that the left (or right) cosets of $G$ partition $G$ by the subgroup $H$. In other words, $G/H = \{gH : g\in G\}$ covers $G$ and the cosets contained are pairwise disjoint.

What is a Normal Subgroup?

A subgroup $H$ of $G$ is normal (written $H \triangleleft G$) if for all $g \in G$, $gHg^{-1} = H$. In other words, $H$ is a subgroup in which left and right cosets are equal. The notation used to say that $H$ is a normal subgroup of $G$ is $H\triangleleft G$.

Normality matters because only then can you multiply cosets and get a well-defined product: $(gH)(hH)=(gh)H$ independent of representatives.

When $H$ is normal the set of cosets $G/H$ is no longer just a set, but is a group (the quotient group).

Example: The subgroup $3\mathbb{Z}$ in $\mathbb{Z}$

$3\mathbb{Z}$ is normal in $\mathbb{Z}$ because addition is commutative:

$$a + (3\mathbb{Z}) - a = 3\mathbb{Z}$$

Example 2: A non-normal subgroup in $S_3$

S3 is the symmetric group on three elements: the group of all permutations of a 3‑element set.
Let $G=S_3=\{e,(12),(13),(23),(123),(132)\}$ and $H=\{e,(12)\}$.

Left cosets:

  • $H=eH=\{e,(12)\}$
  • $(13)H=\{(13),(132)\}$
  • $(23)H=\{(23),(123)\}$

Right cosets:

  • $H=He=\{e,(12)\}$
  • $H(13)=\{(13),(123)\}$
  • $H(23)=\{(23),(132)\}$

The left and right cosets differ here, so $H$ is not normal and you cannot form a quotient group $S_3/H$.

What is a Quotient Group?

A quotient group is a group whose set is the set of all left cosets ($G/H$) of a normal subgroup ($H \triangleleft G$) and whose operation is coset multiplication.

There is a proof showing that if $H$is a normal subgroup ($gH$ = $Hg$ for all $g \in G$), then the multiplication of cosets defined by $(gH)(hH) = (gh)H$ is well-defined. This means that the result does not depend on the choice of representatives from each coset. Thus, $G/H$ with this operation satisfies the group axioms and is indeed a group.

Example: The quotient \(\mathbb{Z}/3\mathbb{Z}\)

  1. Define: Let \(H = 3\mathbb{Z}\), as we have done previously.
  2. List the cosets: There are exactly three distinct cosets. Write \([a]=a+H\). Then

    \([0]=\{\ldots,-6,-3,0,3,6,\ldots\}\)

    \([1]=\{\ldots,-5,-2,1,4,7,\ldots\}\)

    \([2]=\{\ldots,-4,-1,2,5,8,\ldots\}\)

  3. Form the quotient set:
    \[\mathbb{Z}/3\mathbb{Z}=\{[0],[1],[2]\}.\]
  4. Addition of cosets: Define \([a]+[b]=[a+b]\). Example:
    \([1]+[2]=[3]=[0]\).
  5. Why well-defined? If \(a'=a+3m\) and \(b'=b+3n\) then
    \[a'+b'=(a+b)+3(m+n)\in (a+b)+H,\]
    so \([a']+[b']=[a+b]=[a]+[b].\)
  6. Group properties (brief): Identity is \([0]\), inverse of \([a]\) is \([-a]\), and associativity follows from integer addition. Thus \(\mathbb{Z}/3\mathbb{Z}\) is a group of order 3 (indeed cyclic, isomorphic to \(C_3\)).

Remark: because \(\mathbb{Z}\) is abelian every subgroup (including \(3\mathbb{Z}\)) is normal, so the quotient above is a group. In non-abelian groups you must check normality before forming quotients.

What is an Abelian Group?

An abelian group (also called a commutative group) is a group where the operation is commutative. This means for all elements $a, b$ in the group:

$$a \circ b = b \circ a$$

In other words: the order in which you combine elements doesn't matter. This property makes abelian groups easier to work with than general groups.

Example: The integers $\mathbb{Z}$ under addition

$(\mathbb{Z}, +)$ is abelian because addition is commutative:

$$a + b = b + a \quad \text{for all } a, b \in \mathbb{Z}$$

For instance: $3 + 5 = 5 + 3 = 8$.

Non-example: The group of $3 \times 3$ invertible matrices under matrix multiplication is not abelian, since matrix multiplication is not commutative: in general $AB \neq BA$.

What is a Field?

A field is a set $F$ with two operations (addition and multiplication) that satisfy:

  1. $(F, +)$ is an abelian group with identity $0$
  2. $(F \setminus \{0\}, \times)$ is an abelian group with identity $1$
  3. Multiplication distributes over addition: $a \times (b + c) = (a \times b) + (a \times c)$

In simpler terms: you can add, subtract, multiply, and divide (except by zero), and the familiar algebraic rules all hold.

Example: The rational numbers $\mathbb{Q}$

$\mathbb{Q} = \{a/b : a,b \in \mathbb{Z}, b \neq 0\}$ is a field:

  • Addition: $\frac{a}{b} + \frac{c}{d} = \frac{ad + bc}{bd}$ (with identity $0 = \frac{0}{1}$)
  • Multiplication: $\frac{a}{b} \times \frac{c}{d} = \frac{ac}{bd}$ (with identity $1 = \frac{1}{1}$)
  • Inverses: Every nonzero $\frac{a}{b}$ has multiplicative inverse $\frac{b}{a}$

Other familiar examples: $\mathbb{R}$ (real numbers) and $\mathbb{C}$ (complex numbers).

What is a Base Field?

A base field is the field we start with when building larger mathematical structures. When studying a polynomial like $x^2 - 2$, the base field is the field containing the polynomial's coefficients.

The base field determines what numbers we consider "known" versus what we need to "adjoin" (add) to solve the polynomial.

Example: $\mathbb{Q}$ as the base field for $x^2 - 2$

The polynomial $f(x) = x^2 - 2$ has coefficients $1$ and $-2$, which are both rational numbers.

We say $\mathbb{Q}$ is the base field for this polynomial because:

  • The coefficients ($1, -2$) are elements of $\mathbb{Q}$
  • The roots $\pm\sqrt{2}$ are not in $\mathbb{Q}$ (cannot be written as fractions)
  • To solve the polynomial, we must extend $\mathbb{Q}$ by adjoining $\sqrt{2}$

Choosing a different base field would give a different Galois group. For instance, if we chose $\mathbb{R}$ as the base field, the polynomial would already factor since $\sqrt{2} \in \mathbb{R}$.

What is a Field Extension?

A field extension is a larger field $K$ that contains a smaller field $F$ as a subset. We write this as $K/F$.

You can think of this as “adding new elements” (like roots) to $F$ to form $K$.

Example: Adding $\sqrt{2}$ to $\mathbb{Q}$

The field $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{2}) = \{ a + b\sqrt{2} \mid a,b \in \mathbb{Q} \}$ is an extension of $\mathbb{Q}$.

What is a Splitting Field?

For a polynomial $f(x)$ with coefficients in $F$, a splitting field is the smallest field extension $K/F$ in which $f(x)$ factors completely into linear terms.

Example: $f(x) = x^2 - 2$

Over $\mathbb{Q}$, it does not factor. But in $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{2})$, we have:

$$x^2 - 2 = (x - \sqrt{2})(x + \sqrt{2})$$

Thus $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{2})$ is the splitting field of $x^2 - 2$ over $\mathbb{Q}$.

What is an Automorphism?

An automorphism is a bijective map from a structure to itself that preserves its operations. For fields, it preserves addition and multiplication:

$$\sigma(a + b) = \sigma(a) + \sigma(b), \quad \sigma(ab) = \sigma(a)\sigma(b)$$

In a splitting field, automorphisms correspond to symmetries that permute the roots of the polynomial while leaving the coefficients (and thus the polynomial itself) unchanged.

Example: $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{2})$ over $\mathbb{Q}$

The two automorphisms are:

  • $\text{id}$: $\sqrt{2} \mapsto \sqrt{2}$
  • $\sigma$: $\sqrt{2} \mapsto -\sqrt{2}$

Both keep $\mathbb{Q}$ fixed (coefficients unchanged), but $\sigma$ swaps the roots.

What is a Galois Group?

The Galois group of a polynomial $f(x)$ over a field $F$ is the group of all automorphisms of its splitting field $K$ that fix $F$.

In other words: it’s the set of symmetries that permute the roots of $f(x)$ but leave the coefficients (and $F$) unchanged.

Example: $f(x) = x^2 - 2$

The Galois group $\text{Gal}(\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{2}) / \mathbb{Q})$ has two elements:

$$G = \{\text{id}, \sigma\}, \quad \sigma(\sqrt{2}) = -\sqrt{2}$$

This group is isomorphic to $C_2$, the cyclic group of order 2.

What is a Solvable Group?

A group $G$ is solvable if it has a chain of subgroups

$$\{e\} = G_0 \triangleleft G_1 \triangleleft \cdots \triangleleft G_n = G$$

where each $G_i$ is normal in $G_{i+1}$ and each quotient $G_{i+1}/G_i$ is abelian (commutative).

In Galois theory, a polynomial is solvable by radicals if and only if its Galois group is solvable.

Example: The group $\{ \text{id}, \sigma \}$

The only subgroups are $\{\text{id}\}$ and $G$ itself. The quotient $G / \{\text{id}\}$ is abelian, so $G$ is solvable.

Therefore, $x^2 - 2$ is solvable by radicals — as we see from its explicit roots $\pm\sqrt{2}$.

Working Through $f(x) = x^2 - 2$

Step 1: Find the Roots

The polynomial $f(x) = x^2 - 2$ has two roots:

$$r_1 = \sqrt{2}, \quad r_2 = -\sqrt{2}$$

Step 2: Identify the Automorphisms (Symmetries)

We need to find all permutations of the roots that leave the polynomial unchanged (automorphisms of the splitting field).

There are exactly two such permutations:

1. Identity ($\text{id}$):

$$\begin{aligned} \text{id}(\sqrt{2}) &= \sqrt{2} \\ \text{id}(-\sqrt{2}) &= -\sqrt{2} \end{aligned}$$

Leaves everything unchanged.

2. Swap ($\sigma$):

$$\begin{aligned} \sigma(\sqrt{2}) &= -\sqrt{2} \\ \sigma(-\sqrt{2}) &= \sqrt{2} \end{aligned}$$

Swaps the two roots. Corresponds to the map $x \mapsto -x$.

Why does this preserve the polynomial?
If we substitute $-x$ into $f(x) = x^2 - 2$, we get $f(-x) = (-x)^2 - 2 = x^2 - 2 = f(x)$. The polynomial is unchanged!

Step 3: Form the Galois Group

The Galois group $G = \text{Gal}(f)$ is the set of all these automorphisms:

$$G = \{\text{id}, \sigma\}$$

This group has two elements. Let's verify it's actually a group:

  • Closure: Composing any two elements gives another element in $G$
  • Identity: $\text{id}$ is the identity
  • Inverses: $\sigma \circ \sigma = \text{id}$, so $\sigma$ is its own inverse
  • Associativity: Function composition is always associative

Composition table:

$\circ$ $\text{id}$ $\sigma$
$\text{id}$ $\text{id}$ $\sigma$
$\sigma$ $\sigma$ $\text{id}$

Note: This group is isomorphic to $\mathbb{Z}_2$ (integers modulo 2) or $C_2$ (cyclic group of order 2).

Step 4: Find All Subgroups

What are all possible subgroups of $G = \{\text{id}, \sigma\}$?

Remember: A subgroup must:

  • Contain the identity element
  • Be closed under the group operation
  • Contain inverses of all its elements

Subgroup 1: $H_0 = \{\text{id}\}$

The trivial subgroup (just the identity).

Subgroup 2: $H_1 = \{\text{id}, \sigma\} = G$

The whole group.

Why isn't $\{\sigma\}$ a subgroup?

Because it doesn't contain the identity! Also, $\sigma \circ \sigma = \text{id}$, which means composing elements in $\{\sigma\}$ gives us $\text{id}$, which is not in $\{\sigma\}$. So it's not closed under composition.

Step 5: Check if Subgroups are Normal

For each subgroup $H$, we need to check if $gHg^{-1} = H$ for all $g \in G$.

$H_0 = \{\text{id}\}$ is normal:

For any $g \in G$: $g \cdot \text{id} \cdot g^{-1} = g \cdot g^{-1} = \text{id} \in H_0$ ✓

$H_1 = G$ is normal:

The whole group is always a normal subgroup of itself. ✓

Pro tip: In abelian groups (where all elements commute), every subgroup is automatically normal because $gHg^{-1} = gg^{-1}H = H$.

Step 6: Build the Subnormal Series

Now we can construct a chain of normal subgroups:

$$\{\text{id}\} \triangleleft G$$

This gives us one quotient group to check: $G / \{\text{id}\} \cong G$.

Step 7: Check if Quotients are Abelian

For $G$ to be solvable, each quotient $G_{i+1}/G_i$ must be abelian.

Our only quotient is $G/\{\text{id}\} \cong G = \{\text{id}, \sigma\}$.

Is this abelian? Let's check if $\text{id} \circ \sigma = \sigma \circ \text{id}$:

$$\begin{aligned} \text{id} \circ \sigma &= \sigma \\ \sigma \circ \text{id} &= \sigma \\ \therefore \text{id} \circ \sigma &= \sigma \circ \text{id} \quad \checkmark \end{aligned}$$

Yes! The quotient is abelian.

✓ Conclusion: The Group is Solvable!

Since we found a chain of normal subgroups $\{\text{id}\} \triangleleft G$ where the quotient $G/\{\text{id}\}$ is abelian, the Galois group is solvable.

By Galois Theory:

The polynomial $x^2 - 2$ has a closed-form solution expressible using radicals.

$$x = \pm\sqrt{2}$$

Why This Matters

The Big Picture

Galois theory gives us a way to determine a priori (before actually solving) whether a polynomial equation has a closed-form solution.

  • Solvable Galois group → Closed-form solution exists using radicals ($+, -, \times, \div, \sqrt[n]{\cdot}$)
  • Non-solvable Galois group → No closed-form solution using radicals

For Higher Degree Polynomials

The same process applies to polynomials of any degree:

  • Degree 2, 3, 4: Galois groups are always solvable → Quadratic, cubic, quartic formulas exist
  • Degree 5+: Galois groups can be non-solvable (e.g., $S_5$, the symmetric group) → No general formula exists

Key Takeaway

The structure of how roots can be permuted while preserving the polynomial (the Galois group) directly determines whether we can write down an explicit formula for those roots. This deep connection between symmetry and solvability is one of the most beautiful results in mathematics.

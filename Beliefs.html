Introduction

To “believe” something almost seems antiquated. Or rather what it means to believe has at least changed a lot. Humanity's body of knowledge now is so specialized and updates so rapidly that having firm convictions can make one appear weak-minded rather than strong-willed.

There's one framing of this, prevalent in academia and media circles, that sees in our post-modern skepticism the opportunity to move past the quest for objective truth and replace it with a pluralistic "multiple truths" approach. The idea is that, through inclusivity and accepting diverse perspectives, we can create a more harmonious and progressive society.

"Our reverence for the truth might be a distraction that's getting in the way of finding common ground and getting things done...
We all have different truths. They're based on things like where we come from, how we were raised, and how other people perceive us... One of the things you may not now about Wikipedia is that it actually assumes that we are all biased. It is the reason that you are not supposed to write articles about yourselves. "
— Katherine Maher, CEO of NPR, formerly of Wikipedia, in a 2022 Ted Talk

The process of seeking truth and forming beliefs that is so fundamental to individual experience is perceived as a tradeoff against the collective desire for social harmony. Modern liberal democracies are far more repressed and stifling of open debate than their proponents would care to admit. Not only is discussing deeper issues of human nature now perceived as a social faux-pas, and possibly a reason to be "cancelled", but the mechanism by which disagreements are resolved has been entirely overhauled. Debates used to be won by reasoning from first-principles and inspiring faith in listeners. It was taken for granted that To the extent debates still take place today, they are mostly won through "fact-checking" with a quick prompt to a search engine or AI assistant. I would argue that the only genuine debate taking place today is related to the question: Which algorithm should we trust to give us the right answers?

So, both the topics of our debates and the tools we use to resolve them have changed dramatically. Gottfried Leibniz, a man of great faith ironically, predicted a future in which, upon any disagreement, we would just say, “Let us calculate”. It can be argued that we have nearly completed Leibniz's vision of the "Rational Calculator". But, then again, Leibniz also thought this is how God's existence would be proven. And perhaps Godel did in his ontological proof; I certainly wouldn't claim to know. However, I do write an essay about what we can know for certain versus what must be approximated.

Why should one hold beliefs?

So what use are beliefs in a world dominated by data, computation, and, of course, purely secular thought? My beliefs push me to respond to this from within the context of a moral framework; but, for any self-interested individual, there are perhaps two reasons:

  1. The fear-motivated reason: I don’t know about you, but I’d really prefer to not feel mostly regret in my final moments of life. Humans have been working on this for a while, so there is something you can do about it. Whether in the Confessions of the Christian, the Meditations of the Stoic, or the cognitive dissonance analysis of the behavioral therapist, the underlying principle is the same: align one’s actions with one’s values and beliefs. But, the catch is that you must first have beliefs to align with. Without them, you are adrift in a sea of data and opinions, easily swayed by the latest trends or algorithms.
  2. The reward-motivated reason: It pays to have beliefs. If you believe something to be true that others don't, and you turn out to be correct, you can create value for yourself and others. For example, if you truly believe that fiat debt-backed currency regimes are inherently unstable, then you can profit on this belief by holding gold or Bitcoin. Financial instruments function as tools for expressing and testing your beliefs. This is why anywhere that you find a Free Speech society, you will find underneath it a Free Market economy. For better or worse, prediction markets have exploded in popularity because they aggregate and reveal genuine beliefs by linking them to observable outcomes and financial incentives.

Beliefs on Metaphysics

I. The universe is fundamentally intelligible and some truths only seem inaccessible because we ask (mostly) bad questions

Science has made incredible progress in understanding the universe, but there are still many mysteries that remain unsolved. However, I believe that these mysteries are not due to the universe being inherently unknowable, but rather because we have not yet asked the right questions or developed the right tools to uncover the answers. We can't let's say prove everything within a formal axiomatic system let's say but...

When you are considering what you believe, don’t become frustrated that a lot of the bigger more meaningful questions seem unanswerable. It could be that they are not the right questions to ask. No matter how persistently one investigates questions like “where is the edge of the Earth?”, “does God have a white beard”, or “what does the color blue smell like?” a satisfying answer will not be found. I would argue that “what is real?” or “what is the meaning of life?” fall into this category of questions. False assumptions, category errors, loosely defined terms – this is ultimately what keeps us frustrated in our questioning of the world and feeling as though the universe is incomprehensible

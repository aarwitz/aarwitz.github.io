What I Believe (and why to have core beliefs at all)

Published on October 6, 2025

For the modern world, questions about the nature of humanity would be viewed on par with the struggle among the Lilliputians about the correct way to cut open an egg. Hobbes, the first truly modern philosopher, boasted of how he deserted and ran away from fighting in a religious war; a cowardly life had become preferable to a heroic but meaningless death. Dulce et decorum est pro patria mori had been an important part of the old tradition; henceforth, it would be seen as nothing more than an old lie. And so, the Enlightenment undertook a major strategic retreat. If the only way to stop people from killing one another about the right way to open an egg involved a world where nobody thought about it too much, then the intellectual cost of ceasing such thought seemed a small price to pay. The question of human nature was abandoned because it is too perilous a question to debate.”
— Peter Thiel

To “believe” something almost seems antiquated. Humanity's body of knowledge seems to update so rapidly in the modern era that having firm convictions makes you seem weak-minded rather than strong-willed. Debates used to be won by reasoning from first-principles and inspiring faith in listeners, but most of today's debates are won through "fact-checking" with a quick prompt to a search engine or AI assistant.

It is very obvious, to me at least, that the topics of our debates and the tools we use to resolve them have changed dramatically. Gottfried Leibniz, a man of great faith ironically, predicted that in the future we would not argue at all and upon any disagreement we would just say, “Let us calculate”. It can be argued that we have nearly completed Leibniz's vision of the "Rational Calculator". But, then again, Leibniz also thought this is how God's existence would be proven. And perhaps Godel did; I certainly wouldn't claim to know. So what use are beliefs in a world dominated by data, computation, and, of course, purely secular thought?

Well, belief is not about logic or any set of facts. Belief comes from intuition. Belief is something you feel in your gut. You can pull up data points to support your argument in retrospect but ultimately cognitive dissonance is so powerful because people can see so much data to the contrary and still stick to their beliefs, because ultimately we have strong feelings inside of us.

1. The universe is fundamentally intelligible and certain truths only seem inaccessible because we ask (mostly) bad questions.

The arc of history doesn't bend toward justice automatically—it bends because people choose to bend it. Technology doesn't inherently make us better off; human agency and wisdom in how we deploy it does. Every generation faces the choice between stagnation and progress, and that choice is never guaranteed to go the right way.

This means we have both tremendous responsibility and tremendous opportunity. The future isn't predetermined—it's something we actively create through our choices, innovations, and institutions. Pessimism becomes self-fulfilling, but so does thoughtful optimism backed by action.

2. First Principles Thinking Beats Conventional Wisdom

Most of what we accept as "obvious" or "just how things work" is actually contingent—the result of historical accidents, political compromises, or simply what worked in different circumstances. Breaking problems down to their fundamental components and rebuilding from there often reveals better solutions.

This doesn't mean rejecting all tradition or established practice. Rather, it means understanding why things are the way they are, which principles are truly fundamental, and which are merely conventional. The best innovations often come from questioning assumptions everyone else takes for granted.

3. Skin in the Game Reveals Truth

People's opinions matter most when they have something meaningful at stake. The entrepreneur risks their own capital. The engineer's reputation depends on whether the bridge stands. The parent's child's wellbeing depends on their decisions. This alignment between risk and decision-making authority tends to produce better outcomes.

Conversely, be skeptical of advice from those who bear no consequences for being wrong. Academic theories untested by reality, policy proposals from those who won't live with the results, investment advice from those who aren't investing their own money—these often sound good but perform poorly when confronted with the real world.

4. Compound Growth is the Most Powerful Force

Small, consistent improvements compound into extraordinary results over time. This applies far beyond finance—to learning, relationships, health, and personal development. The person who reads for 30 minutes daily will vastly outpace someone who crams for hours occasionally. The company that improves 1% each week will transform completely within a few years.

The corollary is that starting early and being consistent matters more than optimizing for short-term gains. Time is the crucial ingredient that transforms modest efforts into remarkable outcomes. This is why building good systems and habits is often more valuable than pursuing dramatic gestures.

5. Diversity of Thought Requires Intellectual Humility

The best solutions emerge from bringing together different perspectives, experiences, and ways of thinking. But this only works if people are actually willing to change their minds when presented with better evidence or arguments. Intellectual diversity without intellectual humility just creates echo chambers with different voices saying the same things.

This means actively seeking out people who disagree with you—not to debate or convert them, but to understand their perspective and test your own thinking. It means holding your beliefs strongly enough to act on them, but lightly enough to update them when warranted.

6. Focus on Systems, Not Goals

Goals give you direction, but systems give you progress. The person who wants to write a book needs a daily writing habit. The company that wants to innovate needs processes for experimentation and learning from failure. The society that wants prosperity needs institutions that reward productive behavior.

Systems are what remain after the initial motivation fades. They're what carry you through setbacks and uncertainty. A good system makes the right thing to do the easy thing to do, while a bad system makes even people with good intentions produce poor results.

7. Technology Amplifies Human Nature

Every new technology is a tool that can be used for creation or destruction, connection or isolation, enlightenment or manipulation. Social media can build communities or spread misinformation. AI can augment human intelligence or replace human judgment. Nuclear energy can power cities or level them.

This means the crucial questions about any new technology aren't just technical—they're social, political, and moral. How do we structure incentives? What institutions do we need? How do we preserve human agency and dignity while capturing the benefits? The technology is never neutral; what matters is how we choose to deploy it.

Living These Beliefs

Beliefs are only as good as the actions they inspire. These principles push me to take more calculated risks, to question my assumptions regularly, to focus on building systems rather than chasing short-term wins, and to remain optimistic about what's possible while being realistic about what's required to get there.

They're not commandments—they're hypotheses to be tested against reality. Some may prove wrong or incomplete as I gain more experience. But for now, they provide a framework for making decisions and understanding the world that has served me well.

What do you believe? More importantly, how do your beliefs shape your actions? The examined life isn't just about thinking clearly—it's about thinking clearly in service of living well.

What I Believe (and why to have core beliefs at all)

Published on October 6, 2025

For the modern world, questions about the nature of humanity would be viewed on par with the struggle among the Lilliputians about the correct way to cut open an egg. Hobbes, the first truly modern philosopher, boasted of how he deserted and ran away from fighting in a religious war; a cowardly life had become preferable to a heroic but meaningless death. Dulce et decorum est pro patria mori had been an important part of the old tradition; henceforth, it would be seen as nothing more than an old lie. And so, the Enlightenment undertook a major strategic retreat. If the only way to stop people from killing one another about the right way to open an egg involved a world where nobody thought about it too much, then the intellectual cost of ceasing such thought seemed a small price to pay. The question of human nature was abandoned because it is too perilous a question to debate.”
— Peter Thiel

To “believe” something almost seems antiquated. Humanity's body of knowledge seems to update so rapidly in the modern era that having firm convictions makes you seem weak-minded rather than strong-willed. Debates used to be won by reasoning from first-principles and inspiring faith in listeners, but most of today's debates are won through "fact-checking" with a quick prompt to a search engine or AI assistant.

It is very obvious, to me at least, that the topics of our debates and the tools we use to resolve them have changed dramatically. Gottfried Leibniz, a man of great faith ironically, predicted that in the future we would not argue at all and upon any disagreement we would just say, “Let us calculate”. It can be argued that we have nearly completed Leibniz's vision of the "Rational Calculator". But, then again, Leibniz also thought this is how God's existence would be proven. And perhaps Godel did; I certainly wouldn't claim to know. So what use are beliefs in a world dominated by data, computation, and, of course, purely secular thought?

Well, belief is not about logic or any set of facts. Belief comes from intuition. Belief is something you feel in your gut. You can pull up data points to support your argument in retrospect but ultimately cognitive dissonance is so powerful because people can see so much data to the contrary and still stick to their beliefs, because ultimately we have strong feelings inside of us.

1. The universe is fundamentally intelligible and certain truths only seem inaccessible because we ask (mostly) bad questions.
